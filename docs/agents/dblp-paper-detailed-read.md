## A. Transport design

**A1. Data-plane mechanism.** Gradient payloads are UDP; control is TCP-based (p.3, "semi-reliable transmission"). Per packet: "each UDP packet includes a custom header with three fields: (1) a sequence number that uniquely identifies the packet within a single communication round and allows the end host to rearrange out-of-order packets; (2) a round counter to distinguish packets across communication rounds; and (3) the data length" (p.4). Chunk size in bytes is **not stated** — "the size of a chunk and the length of the customized metadata exactly fill up a UDP packet payload. Together with the UDP header, they form an MTU-sized packet" (p.4). The bitmap tracks delivery at one entry per sequence-numbered packet/chunk; exact bit/byte encoding is **not stated**. Flow: sender sends all chunks, then "transmits a probe signal P to the receiver via TCP. The receiver responds with a signal B accompanied by bitmap data, which the sender uses to retransmit only the lost packets. This process repeats until the sender receives a stop signal S" (p.4). The **receiver** decides Stop: "Once received packets reach the bounded threshold, the receiver sends a stop signal" (p.3); Algorithm 2 confirms this happens both on probe arrival and independently. Number of retransmission rounds is **not bounded/stated** — Algorithm 1 loops "while no stop signal do" with no round cap.

**A2. Control plane.** Before training, "DBLP synchronizes all nodes to exchange two constant values: (1) the total number of chunks per communication cycle and (2) the tensor layer names. A single exchange over a reliable channel suffices" (p.4). During training, probe/bitmap/stop signals run over TCP, described as "guaranteed to be delivered through reliable channels" (p.3). Message sizes, timeout values, and probe-interval timing are **not stated** — the probe is sent once per round after all chunks are sent, not on a periodic timer. There is **no statement anywhere** about priority or queueing of control traffic relative to data traffic.

**A3. Congestion control / pacing.** **Not stated, and evidence of absence**: Algorithm 1 (Send) simply iterates over all unsent chunks and sends them with no rate limiting, pacing delay, or window; no text in the paper mentions rate, window, burst-size limiting, ECN, RTT-based reaction, or fairness for the UDP sender. The only place "congestion" appears is describing the *problem* the paper addresses ("transient congestion events," p.1) and citations characterizing DC traffic bursts (pp.5,10) — never a mechanism inside DBLP's sender that reacts to it.

**A4. Loss model in evaluation.** Main testbed experiments: "we simulate microbursts by manually injecting bursty packet loss at rates ∈ {60,70,80,90}%, matching thresholds reported in prior work [40]" (p.5). The exact injection mechanism (tc netem, switch, socket-level drop) is **not stated**. (A separate, earlier preliminary experiment on DenseNet169/CIFAR-10, used only to motivate phase-awareness, says drops were simulated "by randomly zeroing out a fraction of received gradients," p.2 — an application-level drop, not network-level, and distinct from the main microburst evaluation.) Burst extent: 2 injected microburst events for EfficientNetB0 (930 iterations, 10 epochs) and 3 for ResNet50 (1395 iterations, 15 epochs), at randomly chosen iterations (e.g., iters 372/837 for EfficientNetB0-60%; 372/651/1209 for ResNet50) (pp.5–8, Tables II–VI). Each burst affects roughly one round: "the two short-lived microbursts constitute only 0.215% of total communication cycles" (p.9), consistent with 2/930. Whether loss also hits control traffic: **implied no but not stated explicitly for these runs** — control signals are said to be "guaranteed to be delivered through reliable channels" (p.3). Link speeds, NIC, and switch hardware: **not stated** — "the network topology is treated as a black box to our system" (p.4). Topology: 3 worker nodes + 1 server node running "centralized all-reduce" with reduce+broadcast (p.4, Sec. IV-D, V-A1) — matches the belief of a 3-worker/1-server centralized design, though the paper never uses the term "parameter server." GPUs: each of the 3 workers has 1 NVIDIA RTX A4500 (CNN tasks) or 1 NVIDIA RTX 4090 (GPT-2-S) (p.4); the server node's hardware is **not stated**. Model sizes (Table I, p.4): EfficientNetB0 5M params, ResNet50 25M, AlexNet 61M, GPT-2-S 125M.

**A5. Consuming a partial gradient.** Zero-filled, not rescaled or averaged over received chunks only: "Unreceived chunks remain 0-filled. Reconstruct the original data" (Algorithm 2, p.12). No masking metadata is passed to the optimizer beyond this zero-fill; there is **no mention** of error feedback or interplay with compression anywhere in the paper.

## B. The learning-phase policy

**B6. CLR detector.** Rule: relative L2-norm drop between consecutive iterations, "|‖G_prev‖ − ‖G_curr‖| / ‖G_prev‖ ≥ η" with "η = 0.5 following prior work [17]" (Accordion) (p.3, Eq. 1) — matches the stated belief exactly. Detection cadence: **checked once per epoch, not continuously per iteration** — "At the start of each epoch, DBLP checks whether the gradient norm change triggers CLR detection" (p.4); Algorithm 3 confirms: "if at the beginning of an epoch then Reset loss-tolerance threshold to P_high; if CLR is detected then Set loss-tolerance threshold to P_low" (p.12). No hysteresis mechanism and no independent cost measurement are given; the paper only asserts the method is "computationally cheap" by inheriting that characterization from Accordion (p.3). Tolerance is initialized to P_low at the very start of training "as the start of training is indisputably critical" (p.4).

P_low per workload (Table I, p.4): EfficientNetB0 0.8%, ResNet50 2.4%, AlexNet 0.8%, GPT-2-S 0.8% — matches the stated belief exactly. Note: the 0.8%/2.4% values are explicitly attributed to prior work [11] only for EfficientNetB0 and ResNet50 ("EfficientNetB0 tolerates 0.8% loss without accuracy or iteration-count penalties, while ResNet50 tolerates up to 2.4%," p.3); the AlexNet and GPT-2-S values of 0.8% appear only in Table I without independent textual justification — this looks like reuse of the EfficientNetB0 figure rather than a model-specific measurement.

P_high = P_low + k, with default k = 40 percentage points, chosen from a sweep over k ∈ {20,30,40,50,60,70,80}% on EfficientNetB0 (Fig. 2, pp.4–5) — matches the belief. "The accuracy curve in Figure 2b remains flat between 20% and 30% (within 0.001) and then degrades from 40% to 80%... We choose a relatively conservative k=40% as the preferred operating point because it captures a substantial fraction of the available latency reduction (approximately 25% relative to k=20%) while keeping evaluation accuracy above 71%" (p.5). Accuracy evidence for the chosen k=40% appears in Table IX (p.9): EfficientNetB0 DBLP 71.01% vs. baseline 71.30% (99.59% of baseline); ResNet50 82.74% vs. 83.22% (99.42%); AlexNet 70.10% vs. 69.97% (DBLP essentially matches/exceeds); GPT-2-S perplexity 1.6463 (DBLP) vs. 1.5981 (baseline, lower is better).

**B7. Pure-drop tolerance vs. phase (Accordion-style claim).** Demonstrated in one preliminary experiment only: DenseNet169 on CIFAR-10, 9 epochs split into three 3-epoch phases (early/middle/final), with 40% and 80% drop schedules applied to different phases (p.2, Sec. II-C, Fig. 1). "Dropping 40% to 80% of gradients at the middle and final training phases, the model converges at roughly the same rate as when there are no drops. However, dropping gradients at the early stage significantly slows down the convergence rate, resulting in a noticeable accuracy gap" that "does not close by the end of training" (Fig. 1 caption, p.3). This is corroborated by citations to Achille et al. (critical learning periods, [16]) and to gradient-subspace/lottery-ticket work ([30],[31]), but the paper's own supporting evidence is a single model/dataset pair, separate from the four models used in the main evaluation.

## C. Results and their attribution

**C8. Headline numbers.** Abstract/Intro (pp.1–2): average end-to-end training-time reduction 24.8%, maximum 33.9%; up to 5.97× single-round communication latency speedup at microburst events; up to 4.17× lower tail latency and 1.95× lower average latency; DBLP "tolerates 40% additional gradient loss compared to the baseline while achieving comparable evaluation accuracy." Per-model training time (Table VIII, p.9, normalized to DBLP=1): EfficientNetB0 90%-microburst baseline 1.2642 (26.42% DBLP speedup), EfficientNetB0 no-burst baseline 1.1744 (17.44%), ResNet50 70%-microburst baseline 1.1978 (19.78%), ResNet50 no-burst baseline 1.1751 (17.51%), AlexNet baseline 1.3393 (33.9%), GPT-2-S baseline 1.3357 (33.6%). Per-microburst latency tables (pp.7–8, Tables II–VI) give speedups of 4.00×–5.97× at burst iterations and 1.37×–4.17× on tail, 1.52×–1.95× on average latency, across 60–90% loss on EfficientNetB0 and 70% loss on ResNet50. Accuracy cost under bursts (Table VII, p.8): at most 1.55% degradation (EfficientNetB0, 70% loss) and 0.76% (ResNet50, 70% loss).

**Baseline definition — important.** The baseline is **not** stop-and-wait/fully-reliable TCP. It is the *same* semi-reliable UDP-data/TCP-control/bitmap/probe/stop transport as DBLP, but with a single fixed tolerance p throughout training (no CLR-based phase adaptivity), run "in scenarios without switch-level support" (p.8) — a software-only reimplementation of MLT's bounded-loss scheme, without MLT's switch-level priority queuing/dropping. "Despite the claim in [11] that their bounded-loss-tolerance gradient communication scheme resolves the long-tail latency issue, we find that in scenarios without switch-level support and under microbursts, a solution that deploys a fixed small tolerance value collapses" (pp.8–9).

**C9. Decomposing the speedup.** The paper gives only a qualitative decomposition: "At higher loss rates, the baseline's fixed tolerance forces progressively more retransmission rounds, whereas DBLP's higher tolerance allows each communication round to finish early, preventing the buildup of long-tail latency" (p.8). On where the baseline's time goes: "the baseline still receives and processes 99.2% of gradient data at every training iteration (because of the fixed 0.8% tolerance threshold for EfficientNetB0 as an example)... microbursts do not necessarily cause the baseline to converge to lower accuracy: the total percentage of gradients received is unchanged, but the time to deliver all the gradient packets increases dramatically" (p.9). So the attributed mechanism is entirely (c), reaching Stop earlier because the residual-loss target is far looser (40.8% vs 0.8–2.4%), collapsing into fewer probe/bitmap/retransmit round-trips — not fewer *bytes* (both schemes deliver almost all the same bytes per the 99.2% figure), and no separate "avoided timeouts" component is quantified. No byte-count or round-count breakdown table exists; this decomposition is **not stated** at finer granularity.

**C10. Tail latency.** Reported extensively as a summary statistic ("Tail" row in Tables II–VI, e.g., p.7) and visually via per-loss-rate CDFs of send latency (Fig. 4, p.7). However, the precise definition of "tail" (e.g., p95, p99, or simply the maximum observed latency) is **not stated** in the text — the tables just label a row "Tail" without a formula. No variance/standard-deviation statistics are reported, and no straggler analysis beyond the injected-microburst iterations themselves is presented.

## D. Scope and gaps

**D11. RDMA/RoCE, PFC, lossless fabrics, selective retransmission, packet trimming, ECN, DC-scale topologies.** **Not stated anywhere in the paper.** None of "RDMA," "RoCE," "PFC," "ECN," or "lossless" appear in the text at all. "Trimming" appears only inside a bibliography title (ref. [33], on tail latency of packet-processing systems) and is not discussed as a mechanism. The paper explicitly frames hardware-agnosticism as a core design goal, deliberately contrasting with switch-dependent designs: "Hardware agnosticism: the protocol should minimize reliance on configurable network infrastructure, enabling deployment across commodity clusters and cloud environments where switch-level configuration is infeasible" (p.1), and characterizes prior work (MLT) as relying on "inter-packet dependency and priority-based packet queuing and dropping on switches... which violates" hardware agnosticism (p.1). The network is explicitly treated as an opaque black box (p.4). There is no discussion of a lossless/PFC fabric or of selective-repair-capable switches; DBLP's own bitmap-based selective retransmission is a transport-layer analogue but the paper never connects it to UEC-style fabric selective repair or trimming.

**D12. Stated limitations/future work (Section VI, p.9, plus scattered notes).** (a) Only data parallelism was studied: "we adopt data parallelism and leave other schemes [tensor, pipeline, expert parallelism] to future work" (p.2). (b) Only a 3-worker/1-server testbed: "Evaluating DBLP at larger scales is left for future work" (p.4). (c) "Bounded-loss Cross-DC Collectives" — combining topology-aware NCCL-style optimization with DBLP's phase-adaptive reliability is proposed but not built (p.9). (d) "Network Scheduling" — using CLR signal to drive DC-level flow prioritization/bandwidth allocation is proposed but not implemented (p.9). (e) Model/scale limits: "Our experiments focus on CNNs and a small-scale language model due to hardware constraints, but the protocol behavior generalizes across model architectures" (p.5) — a claim of generalization not backed by larger-model evidence. (f) "We hypothesize that the microburst improvements would be even more pronounced at values of k>40%, which we leave as a direction for future investigation" (p.8).

**D13. GPU idle time / cost of communication.** DBLP does not report its own GPU-idle-time metric; it reports "send latency" per iteration as the proxy for communication cost. Cited third-party figures used to motivate the work: PipeDream — "training over 32 GPUs resulted in 90% of total training time being spent on communication" (p.2); COMET — "communication accounted for 47% of the execution time in the forward pass" (p.1); ByteScheduler — "end-to-end performance improvement does not scale linearly with the number of GPUs due to communication bottlenecks" (p.2). Architecturally, Algorithm 4 (Worker, p.12) shows a fully synchronous, blocking per-iteration loop — forward/backward pass, then blocking DBLP_SEND, then blocking DBLP_RECV, then parameter update — with **no computation/communication overlap** implemented in DBLP itself, meaning the measured send-latency numbers effectively equal per-iteration GPU idle/stall time in this testbed.

## What this means for a modern-fabric simulation

- DBLP's headline speedups are measured against a baseline that is itself a bitmap/probe/stop UDP transport with a fixed low tolerance and **no switch support** (p.8) — not against a standard RDMA/RoCEv2 reliable-transport baseline, so its "up to 5.97×" numbers are not directly comparable to a UEC-fabric baseline.
- The core speedup mechanism the paper describes — fewer probe/bitmap/retransmit round trips before Stop — is transport-specific: it exists because the baseline's *loss-chasing* protocol becomes expensive precisely when the fabric offers no cheap way to know what's missing or to retransmit it selectively and fast. A fabric with fast per-packet loss signaling and cheap selective repair collapses the round-trip cost DBLP is optimizing away.
- DBLP's own retransmission is already "selective" (per-packet bitmap), but at the *application/transport* layer using round-trip TCP probes, not link-layer NACK/trim signaling — this is exactly the gap a UEC/trimming fabric closes natively and cheaply.
- No congestion control, pacing, or ECN reaction exists in DBLP's sender (p.4, Algorithm 1) — DCQCN/NSCC-style CC is simply absent from this design; any interaction between phase-aware tolerance and modern CC is unstudied.
- The paper never discusses RDMA, RoCE, PFC, ECN, packet trimming, or lossless fabrics (p.1, p.9) — its "hardware agnosticism" goal was explicitly framed as avoiding switch dependence, which is a different axis from "the fabric already does selective, low-latency repair."
- The zero-fill consumption of missing gradient chunks (p.12) is transport-independent — it is a property of how DBLP treats incomplete data at the application layer, and would hold regardless of the underlying fabric.
- The CLR detector and the P_low/P_high phase-budget policy (Eq. 1, Table I, p.3–4) are entirely transport-independent: they are a training-dynamics signal and a bound on acceptable staleness/loss, applicable to any transport that can honor a target completion fraction.
- The bounded-loss "Stop once missing fraction ≤ P" completion semantic (p.3) is also transport-independent in principle — any fabric could be asked to honor the same target, but the *value* of stopping early shrinks if the fabric can already deliver a near-complete gradient cheaply and fast via selective repair.
- The paper's own evidence that "the baseline still receives and processes 99.2% of gradient data at every training iteration" even during a microburst (p.9) suggests the *byte cost* of full delivery is not the bottleneck in their setup — the round-trip/timeout structure of their control loop is. This is precisely the property a fabric with cheap loss notification would remove.
- Evaluation scale is small (3 workers, 5–125M-parameter models, p.4) and topology-agnostic ("black box," p.4) — no evidence exists at datacenter scale or on RDMA-class hardware, so extrapolating DBLP's numeric speedups to a UEC/ASTRA-sim fabric is unsupported by the paper itself.
- CLR detection is per-epoch, not per-iteration (p.4, Algorithm 3) — a coarser cadence than the phase-adaptivity framing might suggest; this affects how quickly the phase-budget policy could react to fabric-level events even in principle.
- No tail-latency percentile definition, no byte/round decomposition of the speedup, and no error-feedback/compression interplay are given (pp.7–9) — several of the programme's specific attribution questions are genuinely unanswered by the paper and would need to be independently derived in simulation.
- AlexNet's and GPT-2-S's P_low = 0.8% values are tabulated without independent justification (p.4, Table I) — likely inherited from the EfficientNetB0 figure rather than measured per-model, weakening the generality of the phase-budget numbers themselves.
