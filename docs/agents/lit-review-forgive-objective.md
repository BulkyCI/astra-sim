# What objective should decide when a receiver spends a byte of tolerance

Written 2026-09-17 by a single Opus 5 agent at lite effort, serial reading, for Joe Fang. Given that Joe's FORGIVE protocol fixes the tolerance at `p = 0.1` of owed bytes per rank and per step, this review asks what the receiver should maximise when it chooses which trims to forgive. Forgiveness buys no time at the instant it happens, since the licence reads the sum of forgiven bytes and outstanding holes; arms forgiving 7.55 % and 5.5 % of bytes both recovered about 16 %, and the gap to an arm that never re-engages the congestion controller is about 3 points. The sources are disjoint from the budget-shape review of the same date.

## Irreversible spend with a deferral option

Because an irreversible expenditure destroys the option to wait for information, Dixit and Pindyck require the firm to count that destroyed option as part of the cost of investing, so the threshold that justifies committing exceeds the one a net-present-value calculation alone would give. Their conditions are three: the spend cannot be undone, the payoff is uncertain, and waiting resolves some of that uncertainty. FORGIVE meets all three, since nothing forgiven is refunded, the payoff depends on whether the repair would be trimmed again, and one round trip reveals the repair's fate. The receiver should therefore require a strictly positive expected yield before forgiving, not a merely non-negative one.

Ski rental states the null hypothesis. A deterministic rule that rents until accumulated rent equals the purchase price `B` and then buys attains `2 - 1/B`, which no deterministic rule beats, and a randomised threshold attains about `e/(e-1) = 1.58`; both depend on the rent being strictly positive, since the rent is what the buyer escapes by committing. FORGIVE pays no rent, because a deferred trim stays an outstanding hole and the licence prices that hole exactly as it prices a forgiven byte. If the rent is zero, the threshold rule never buys; ski rental therefore predicts that the receiver should forgive nothing except where some other channel pays, which is the zero marginal time per forgiven byte that Joe measured.

Emek, Kutten and Wattenhofer attain `O(log^2 n + log Delta)` for min-cost matching with delays, and Azar, Ganesh, Ge and Panigrahi attain `O(log^4 n)` for online service with delay; both price metric distance plus accumulated waiting time, and both need a batching discount FORGIVE lacks, so only their instruction transfers, which is to commit once the accrued cost of not committing matches the cost of committing.

## Threshold policies on a system state, priced by a multiplier

Liu, Chong and Shroff serve the user whose channel-state metric, offset by a per-user Lagrange multiplier, is largest under a resource-sharing constraint, and they report 20 % to 150 % over a channel-blind scheduler. Ozel and co-authors give the same structure for a budget arriving over time, since their directional water-filling algorithm allocates power to a constant level except where causality binds, energy flowing only forward in time, or where the finite battery binds.

Both map onto FORGIVE with one sign reversed, because the wireless transmitter spends when the channel is good whereas the FORGIVE receiver would spend when the fabric is bad; the reversal changes the metric and not the structure, since both spend where the marginal yield per unit of budget exceeds a water level. Forgiveness cannot flow backward in time, which is causality, and the receiver cannot spend beyond what the soft vesting cap has released, which is battery capacity. The prescribed rule is `forgive the trim if q_t >= lambda`, where `q_t` estimates the probability that this flow's repair would be trimmed again and `lambda` is the shadow price of the remaining budget, raised when spend runs ahead of the vesting line and lowered when it falls behind.

## Whether repairs are in fact trimmed again

Phanishayee and co-authors supply the only direct measurement this review found. They define a timeout category for the case in which "a retransmitted packet triggered by TCP's loss recovery mechanism is also dropped", and their Table 3, at sixteen servers, a sixty-four-packet switch buffer and a 256 KB server request unit, counts 61 lost retransmits among 589 timeouts under Reno, 2 among 442 under NewReno, and 41 among 349 under NewReno at a duplicate-ACK threshold of one. Those shares, 10.4 %, 0.5 % and 11.7 %, count timeout events and not retransmitted packets, so they are not the quantity FORGIVE needs; they do show that the phenomenon exists and varies by an order of magnitude with the recovery mechanism. Vasudevan and co-authors attribute the majority of incast timeouts to full window losses and report barrier-synchronised throughput falling by up to 90 %, without any share for retransmissions lost again.

The trimming line designs the phenomenon out. Handley and co-authors show that Cut Payload suffers a congestion collapse in which "the mean goodput of the CP flows decreases, as an increasing fraction of the link is occupied by trimmed packet headers"; NDP removes it by pacing every repair through a receiver pull queue, "pacing their arrival to keep its link saturated", and Olteanu and co-authors generalise that credit scheme in EQDS. Under either design a repair is never injected faster than the receiver can absorb it, so the repeat-trim rate is near zero by construction and neither paper reports it. Because Joe's fabric runs UEC-style selective repeat with DCQCN and no receiver credit, repairs re-enter the saturated fabric unpaced, so the repeat-trim rate is a property of this configuration that no prior paper has measured; on the number bounding the load channel, no source exists.

## Utility per unit of budget when the utility is time

Sidiroglou-Douskos and co-authors filter out loops whose perforation is unacceptable, then search the survivors for Pareto-optimal policies, reporting gains typically above two times and up to seven times for accuracy changes under 10 %; because both phases are offline, loop perforation decides where to spend and not when. Baek and Chilimbi give the closer shape, since Green calibrates a model of the QoS loss an approximation produces, applies it under the programmer's constraint, and re-estimates both at runtime.

No loss-tolerant transport found here gates its spend on the probability that the repair itself would be lost. OptiReduce bounds each AllReduce stage under an adaptive timeout, which gates loss on elapsed time; the APNet 2025 bounded-gradient-loss work sets a predefined threshold per round from estimated per-worker loss rates, which gates on measured loss and not on the current trim ratio. Beyond MLT, already covered by the budget-shape review, no source exists.

## A bound whose value is in never being reached

Fowler records the one design convention this review found: because a trip signals an abnormal condition, "usually you'll also want some kind of monitor alert if the circuit breaker trips". The convention presumes a rare trip and a breaker whose worth is the bound it guarantees, not any throughput it saves. The hard cap `p x owed` is FORGIVE's breaker, and no objective should reward approaching it.

## What the objective should be

This review recommends (b), utility per forgiven byte, with the prefix bound of (c) kept as a feasibility constraint and never promoted to the objective, and with a strictly positive reserve price so that the receiver declines when the estimated yield is zero. Dixit and Pindyck require a hurdle above break-even for any irreversible spend with an uncertain payoff, so the reserve price is the correct threshold and not a tuning convenience; opportunistic scheduling and directional water-filling both yield `spend when the state metric exceeds a multiplier`, priced by the remaining budget, and Green runs that structure against a hard quality bound.

Because Joe measured the marginal time per forgiven byte at zero, (b) degenerates at the current operating point into forgiving almost nothing, which is what (a) prescribes when loss is minimised second; the two agree on this fabric and disagree only if the repeat-trim rate is materially above zero. Promoting (c) to the objective is wrong on its own terms, since Joe's vesting rule already guarantees the prefix bound by construction, so restating that guarantee invites the breaker-as-target error.

## The one experiment that decides it

Run seven arms at `p = 0.1` on the worst cell, with Yashar's coin at `P = 0.25` wherever a coin appears. The control is Joe's current state-blind vesting rule, and the (a) arm forgives only the minimum that keeps forgiven plus outstanding below `p x owed`, deferring every other trim. Two (b) arms forgive a trim only when the receiver's running per-flow estimate of the repeat-trim probability exceeds `lambda`, with `lambda` set by a dual update against the vesting line and initialised near zero in one and at the fabric's mean trim ratio in the other. The (c) arm spends to keep the prefix loss share as close to `p` as the bound permits at every prefix. The never-re-engage arm supplies the ceiling and the zero-tolerance arm the floor.

The deciding quantity is the repeat-trim rate, defined per flow and per step as the repairs that are themselves trimmed divided by the repairs sent; the simulator already counts per-flow repairs and repeated trims in the bundle. If the control's repeat-trim rate stays below about 1 %, the load channel is null, and both the (a) arm and the low-`lambda` (b) arm should match the control on worst all-reduce time at strictly lower DP loss, which decides for (a). If the rate exceeds a few per cent, the high-`lambda` (b) arm should beat the (a) arm on time at equal loss, which decides for (b). The (c) arm should never beat the control on time, and if it does, the load model in this review is wrong.
