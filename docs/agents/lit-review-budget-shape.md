# The shape of a spend allowance over an interval, and what FORGIVE should do with it

Written 2026-09-17 by a single Opus 5 agent at lite effort, serial reading, for Joe Fang. The measured front bias it answers, from run #127 flow telemetry bucketed by flow start within the step: under linear vesting at budget 0.1 the soft cap forgives 18 % of trim bytes in the first fifth of a step and 60 % in the last (run #127, `p01_single`, seed 9550582); under the coin at 0.25 the shares are 65 % to 100 %, and under the up-front cap 97 % to 13 %.

Joe's FORGIVE protocol gives each receiving rank a per-step loss budget of `p` of the bytes it is owed, and Joe's vesting rule releases that budget along a line through the origin in received-bytes space, reaching `p x owed` exactly when `(1 - p) x owed` has arrived. Yashar suggests starting that line at a non-zero value, and Zechen objects that a line through the origin is biased toward the back of the step, since a trim arriving in the opening incast meets an allowance of nearly zero while a late trim meets one that is nearly full. This review asks what prior work on budgets spent over an interval says about the shape of the allowed-spend curve, and it reports where it says nothing.

## Pacing literature: the reference curve tracks forecast demand, not the clock

The finding that recurs across the advertising pacing work is that the target cumulative spend is proportional to a forecast of the opportunities, not to elapsed time. Agarwal, Ghosh, Wei and You set the target cumulative spend for campaign `i` before time `t` at `budget x f_{i,t} / f_{i,T}`, where `f_{i,t}` forecasts the cumulative eligible impressions, and their controller then throttles probabilistically against that target (the formula comes from a third-party summary of the KDD 2014 paper, since both hosted PDFs refused the fetch, so it is second-hand). Xu, Lee, Li, Qi and Lu build the same principle into Smart Pacing, learning each campaign's delivery pace from offline and online data so that spend follows the demand pattern rather than a flat line. Balseiro, Kim, Mahdian and Mirrokni place Yashar's coin in this family, comparing probabilistic throttling against bid shading and four other budget management strategies by the system equilibrium each induces, and Balseiro and Gur derive the adaptive pacing strategy that tracks a target spend rate by dual descent.

This literature reframes the disagreement. Because FORGIVE's cap uses received bytes rather than time as its argument, Joe's line is not a clock-uniform schedule; it is a schedule uniform in delivered work, and if trims arrived in proportion to delivered bytes it would already be the curve this literature recommends. Zechen's objection is precisely the claim that they do not, since the incast at a collective's opening concentrates trims at the front. Stated that way, the fix the advertising work prescribes is not an intercept but the `f_{i,t} / f_{i,T}` substitution, indexing the cap by the forecast trim profile.

## Error budgets: the uniform line is a reference, not an enforcement

Google's SRE workbook fixes the uniform line as its reference: with a 99.9 % SLO over 30 days, a constant 0.1 % error rate uses exactly all of the error budget, a burn rate of 1. The practice enforces nothing, since the full budget is available from the first second and the recommended alerts tolerate 2 % of it burned within one hour; SRE therefore argues for the up-front cap rather than for vesting. The analogy fails here, because forgiveness is never refunded whereas an error budget refills every window, and Joe already measured the up-front cap at 9 to 10 % of time recovered against 16 % with vesting (run #127).

## Initial allowances: sized by burst physics, never as a fraction of the total

Three standards give Yashar's intercept a precedent, and all three size it the same way. RFC 2697 states that the token buckets C and E are initially full, `Tc(0) = CBS` and `Te(0) = EBS`, with CBS chosen as the burst the policer intends to admit rather than as a share of the committed rate. RFC 6928 raises TCP's initial window to `min(10 x MSS, max(2 x MSS, 14600))`, and its authors defend the number empirically, reporting that ten segments gave most of the benefit without a significant increase in retransmission rates. Kung and Morris require credit at least the link cell rate times the round-trip propagation delay, so that one active virtual circuit can fill the link. If Joe adopts an intercept, the instruction is to set it to the trims expected within roughly one round trip of the collective's opening rather than to a round fraction of `p x owed`.

## Non-linear release: one formal precedent, and it is front-loaded

Equity vesting offers the vocabulary but little else: the practitioner sources describe cliff vesting as an award that vests in its entirety on a date, graded vesting as release in tranches, and the cliff as a screen against short tenure, which produces a back-loaded payoff; no source in this line studies a concave release schedule. The formal precedent comes from w-event differential privacy, where Budget Distribution allocates the privacy budget in an exponentially decreasing way across the timestamps at which a publication occurs, spending `eps/4, eps/8, ..., eps/2^(m+1)` where the uniform baseline spends `eps/w` at every timestamp. That schedule is concave in cumulative terms, it front-loads because an early release is worth more and because expiring budget is recycled back, and it keeps the invariant `sum over the window of eps_tau <= eps` by construction rather than by monitoring; it is the closest published instance of Joe's candidate (b), with the structure Joe needs, since the shape is free and the endpoint is not.

## Loss-tolerant transports do not shape the allowance at all

MLT synchronises a per-tensor loss-tolerant bound `p` before training and tolerates up to a `p` fraction of packet losses without retransmission; that bound is a single end-of-transfer threshold, and MLT differentiates tolerance across layers rather than across time (the receiver's stop-at-`(1 - p)` rule is from memory, since both hosted PDFs defeated text extraction and only the abstract was read). OptiReduce replaces run-to-completion stages with time-bounded ones under an adaptive timeout, which makes its loss allowance a deadline; a deadline sacrifices the entries that arrive last, so OptiReduce is maximally back-loaded in arrival time, and its authors accept that because the resulting loss stays under 0.1 % of traffic. Weintraub, Banner and Orda give the reason this matters beyond fairness as an aesthetic: their unbiasedness argument reconstructs a gradient estimate from whatever packets arrive under random loss, validated at 10 % random packet loss, and forgiveness concentrated in the last quarter of every step is not random loss, since it selects the same late senders step after step while the sizes repeat. No loss-tolerant transport found here shapes a loss allowance over arrival time, and no source defines fairness across arrival time for a bounded budget; on both, the answer is no source.

## The three candidate shapes as receiver rules

Yashar's intercept becomes `forgiven <= c + ((p x owed - c) / ((1 - p) x owed)) x received`. It guarantees the end-of-step invariant by construction and needs only the constant `c`; its weakness is the one the up-front cap already demonstrated at full scale, since `c` is spendable at byte zero, so a heavy opening burst consumes it at once and the receiver returns to the line, which helps the first trims rather than early trims generally.

A concave release becomes `forgiven <= p x owed x g(received / ((1 - p) x owed))` with `g` increasing, `g(0) = 0` and `g(1) = 1`, for instance `g(x) = x^a` with `a < 1`. The invariant survives because `g(1) = 1`, the rule needs no new state, and the allowance near the origin grows steeply while never being spendable all at once, which is the property the intercept lacks.

Indexing by the trim profile becomes `forgiven <= p x owed x F(received)`, where `F` is the empirical fraction of the previous step's trims that had occurred by that point in received bytes. Under this rule the allowance released by any point equals the share of the step's trims expected by that point, so a trim's chance of meeting an available allowance is equalised across arrival position by construction, which states the fairness Joe asked for as an equation. It needs the previous step's trim profile, which the receiver already has, it preserves the invariant because `F(1) = 1`, and it degrades to Joe's current line when trims are uniform in delivered bytes; its risk is profile drift between steps, which a clamp of `F` from below by the linear line and from above by a constant such as 0.9 would bound.

## Recommendation and the one experiment

Joe should adopt the profile-indexed cap, keep Yashar's coin at `P = 0.25`, and keep the concave `x^a` rule as the fallback that needs no history. The pacing literature's consistent finding is that the allowed-spend curve should follow the forecast opportunity profile rather than any fixed geometry, Budget Distribution shows that a front-loaded release keeps a hard invariant by construction, and the intercept alone is the weakest of the three because a burst spends it at once.

One wave settles it. At `p = 0.1` and `P = 0.25` on the worst cell, run seven arms: Joe's linear vesting as control, the up-front cap as the known-bad reference, the intercept at `c = 0.1 x p x owed` and at `c = 0.25 x p x owed` with the slope readjusted, the concave rule at `a = 0.5` and at `a = 0.25`, and the profile-indexed rule taking `F` from the previous step. The quantity that decides the fairness claim is the share of soft refusals falling in the first 25 % of each step's received bytes, divided by the share of that step's trims occurring there; a fair shape drives that ratio toward one, whereas Joe's current line should show it well above one. The headline axes stay as they are, and every arm must certify `forgiven <= p x owed` in every step.

## Appendix: the same problem in the online-allocation literature

Added 2026-09-17 by a single Opus 5 agent, serial reading, from five papers retrieved in full text. Joe's rule allocates a fixed budget irrevocably, in arrival order, to arrivals whose total is unknown, and the online-allocation literature states that problem as a theorem.

Sean Sinclair, Gauri Jain, Siddhartha Banerjee and Christina Lee Yu (Joe's citation omits Jain) prove the contention. Their Theorem 4.2 states that any online algorithm which achieves counterfactual envy `Delta_EF <= L_T = o(1)` with probability at least `1 - alpha` must also incur waste `Delta_efficiency >= C~ min{sqrt(T), 1/L_T}`, where `T` counts the rounds and the constant `C~` depends on the demand variances `sigma^2_min` and `sigma^2_max`. Their Guarded-Hope algorithm attains that frontier, achieving `Delta_EF <~ max{1/sqrt(T), L_T}` and `Delta_efficiency <~ min{sqrt(T), 1/L_T}` with probability `1 - delta`, given confidence bounds on future arrivals per type. The guardrail solves the Eisenberg-Gale program twice against adjusted demand estimates, producing an upper allocation `X̄` and a lower allocation `X`, and it grants the upper amount only when the remaining budget exceeds what `X` reserves for expected future arrivals plus a confidence term. Joe's line through the origin is therefore not the guardrail, which gives an early arrival the lower allocation at once and withholds only the excess.

Vahideh Manshadi, Rad Niazadeh and Scott Rodilitz solve the version closest to FORGIVE. Their PPA policy allocates `x_i = min{d_i, s_i d_i / (d_i + mu_{i+1})}`, where `s_i` is the remaining supply and `mu_{i+1}` the conditional expectation of future demand. Their Theorem 1 shows that no sequential policy attains an ex-post fairness guarantee above `kappa_p(mu, n)`, equal to `1 - (n / (2(n+1))) mu` for `mu < 1`, to `mu - (n / (2(n+1))) mu^2` for `mu` in `[1, (n+1)/n)`, and to `(n+1) / (2n)` above that; their Theorem 2 shows PPA attains it, and the paper states that the infimum of PPA's guarantee equals 0.5 as `n` grows without bound. The conditions are arbitrarily correlated demands from a known joint distribution, an irrevocable one-time fill rate, and a planner knowing only conditional first moments. The profile-indexed cap recommended above is PPA with received bytes supplying `mu_{i+1}`, and PPA is not Joe's line, since it splits the remaining supply between current and forecast future demand and so funds the first arrival at a positive rate under any scarcity.

Santiago Balseiro, Haihao Lu and Vahab Mirrokni give the price form. Their algorithm updates the dual price by `mu_{t+1} = argmin_mu g_t^T mu + (1/eta) V_h(mu, mu_t)` with gradient `g_t = -b_t(x~_t) + rho`, accepting the action that maximises `f_t(x) - mu_t^T b_t(x)` while resources remain. Their Theorem 1 bounds regret by `C_1 + C_2 eta T + C_3 / eta`, so a step size of order `T^(-1/2)` yields `O(sqrt(T))` regret against the hindsight optimum under i.i.d. requests from an unknown distribution, matching the lower bound, and their Theorem 2 attains an asymptotically optimal fixed competitive ratio under adversarial input, in one pass and without convexity. Joe's line is the price path this result rules out, since the price it implies is unbounded at the start of a step and zero at the end, whereas dual descent raises the price only once spend runs ahead of `rho`; Yashar's coin is a constant price, which explains its even 65 to 100 % coverage.

Yunhong Zhou, Deeparnab Chakrabarty and Rajan Lukose define the threshold `Psi(z) = (Ue/L)^z (L/e)` on the fraction `z` of the knapsack filled, accept item `t` when `v(t)/w(t) >= Psi(z(t))`, and prove in their Theorem 3.2 a competitive ratio of `ln(U/L) + 1`, optimal for efficiencies in `[L, U]`; because `Psi(z) <= L` for `z <= 1 / (1 + ln(U/L))`, their algorithm accepts everything until that fraction of the budget is spent, so its threshold rises with spend, not with elapsed demand. Jeffrey Vitter's Algorithm R makes the `(t+1)`st record a candidate with probability `n/(t+1)`, and "the candidate it replaces is chosen randomly from the n candidates", so equal inclusion probability is bought by revoking an earlier selection.

This literature tells Joe to index the cap by a forecast of the remaining trims, as PPA does, and to let the price rise with spend rather than fall with arrival position, as dual descent and `Psi(z)` both do. It cannot price the licence headroom, because every policy above allocates a good whose value does not depend on when it was granted, whereas an early forgiveness converts a hole that would otherwise drain into permanent spend.
